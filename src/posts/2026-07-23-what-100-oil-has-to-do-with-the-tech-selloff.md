One of the most interesting things about financial markets is how events that initially seem unrelated can quickly become connected.

On July 23, Brent crude oil climbed above $100 per barrel as escalating tensions in the Middle East raised concerns about global oil supplies. At the same time, technology stocks fell sharply, with the Nasdaq dropping more than 2%. Alphabet and Tesla were among the biggest contributors to the decline.

At first glance, an increase in oil prices and a selloff in technology stocks may appear to be two separate stories. But I think the connection between them provides an important lesson about how markets work.

Higher oil prices affect much more than the energy sector. Oil is a major input throughout the global economy, influencing transportation, manufacturing, shipping, aviation, and ultimately the prices consumers pay for many goods and services.

When oil prices rise significantly, investors begin worrying that inflation could increase again.

That creates a chain reaction:

**Higher oil prices → greater inflation risk → higher interest-rate expectations → higher bond yields → pressure on stock valuations.**

Technology and other growth stocks can be particularly sensitive to higher interest rates because much of their valuation depends on profits investors expect them to generate years into the future. When interest rates rise, those future earnings become less valuable in today's dollars.

But interest rates were only part of the pressure on technology stocks.

Investors are also becoming increasingly focused on how much large technology companies are spending on artificial intelligence—and whether those investments will eventually generate enough profit to justify the cost.

Alphabet, for example, indicated that its capital expenditures could reach approximately $205 billion this year. The company continues to invest heavily in AI infrastructure, but investors are beginning to ask a more difficult question: **What return will companies ultimately earn on these enormous investments?**

Tesla faced its own concerns after reporting negative free cash flow, contributing to a sharp decline in its shares.

For me, the biggest lesson from this market move is not simply that oil went up and technology stocks went down. It is that financial markets are interconnected.

A geopolitical event involving oil tankers in the Middle East can affect global energy prices. Higher energy prices can influence inflation expectations. Inflation expectations can change assumptions about Federal Reserve policy and interest rates. And those changing interest rates can ultimately affect how investors value a technology company in Silicon Valley.

Understanding those connections is one of the reasons I find investing and economics so interesting.

Markets are not just numbers moving on a screen. They reflect a constantly changing relationship between businesses, governments, consumers, geopolitics, interest rates, and investor expectations.
