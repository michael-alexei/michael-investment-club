When I first became interested in investing, I focused mostly on stock prices. If a stock was rising, it was easy to assume the company was doing well. Over time, I realized that analyzing an investment requires looking much deeper than the price on a screen.

Before I consider investing in a company, I try to understand several things.

### 1. Understand the Business

I start with a basic question: **How does this company actually make money?**

I look at its products or services, its customers, its major sources of revenue, and the industry in which it operates. If I cannot clearly explain how the business works, I probably do not understand it well enough to invest in it.

### 2. Look at Growth

Next, I examine whether the company is growing.

Revenue growth can tell me whether demand for the company's products or services is increasing. I also look at earnings and whether profitability is improving over time.

However, growth alone does not necessarily make a company a good investment. I want to understand what is driving that growth and whether it appears sustainable.

### 3. Evaluate Profitability and Cash Flow

A company can report impressive revenue growth while still struggling financially.

That is why I look beyond sales and examine profitability, margins, and cash flow. Free cash flow is particularly interesting to me because it can provide insight into how much cash a business generates after making the investments necessary to operate and grow.

### 4. Identify the Competitive Advantage

I also ask: **Why would customers choose this company instead of a competitor?**

A strong brand, innovative technology, network effects, intellectual property, scale, or customer loyalty can give a business an advantage that is difficult for competitors to replicate.

The stronger and more durable that advantage is, the more interesting the company becomes to me.

### 5. Consider the Valuation

One of the most important lessons I have learned is that **a great company is not automatically a great investment**.

Price matters.

Even an outstanding business can be a poor investment if investors are already paying an extremely high price based on unrealistic expectations.

I try to compare a company's valuation with its growth, profitability, competitors, and historical performance rather than looking at the stock price by itself.

### 6. Think About What Could Go Wrong

Finally, I consider the risks.

What happens if economic growth slows? What if interest rates remain high? Could a competitor introduce a better product? Is the company dependent on one market or product? Could regulation affect the business?

Thinking about risk forces me to challenge my own investment thesis instead of searching only for information that supports it.

### My Biggest Takeaway

The more I learn about investing, the more I realize that investing is not about predicting which stock will rise tomorrow.

For me, it is about understanding businesses, evaluating information, questioning assumptions, and making decisions when the outcome is uncertain.

That process is one of the reasons I find investing so interesting. Every company presents a different puzzle, and analyzing it requires combining finance, economics, strategy, psychology, and an understanding of what is happening in the world.
